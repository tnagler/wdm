\section{Weighted empirical framework}
\label{sec:framework}

\subsection{Observations and normalized case weights}

Let $(X_i,Y_i)$, $i=1,\ldots,n$, denote the observed pairs and let
$w_i\geq 0$ be their case weights, with at least one positive weight. Write

\begin{align*}
  W_n &= \sum_{i=1}^n w_i,
  &
  p_i &= \frac{w_i}{W_n}.
\end{align*}

The normalized weights satisfy $\sum_i p_i=1$ and define the weighted
empirical measure

\begin{align*}
  \mathbb{P}_{n,w}
  &= \sum_{i=1}^n p_i\,\delta_{(X_i,Y_i)},
  &
  \mathbb{P}_{n,w}f
  &= \sum_{i=1}^n p_i f(X_i,Y_i).
\end{align*}

All coefficients considered below depend on the weights only through the
$p_i$. They are therefore invariant under multiplication of all weights by a
common positive constant. An observation with zero weight has zero mass and
is treated as absent. The weights are case masses, not weights attached to
particular rank positions; this distinction matters because ``weighted rank
correlation'' is also used for coefficients that deliberately emphasize the
top or bottom of a ranking \citep{shieh1998}.

The concentration of the case masses is summarized by Kish's effective sample
size \citep{kish1965},

\begin{align*}
  n_{\mathrm{eff}}
  &= \frac{\left(\sum_{i=1}^n w_i\right)^2}
           {\sum_{i=1}^n w_i^2}
   = \frac{1}{\sum_{i=1}^n p_i^2}.
\end{align*}

If the observations are independent and identically distributed, the weights
are held fixed, and $h(X_i,Y_i)$ has variance $\sigma_h^2$, then

\begin{align*}
  \operatorname{Var}\!\left(
    \mathbb{P}_{n,w}h\mid p_1,\ldots,p_n
  \right)
  &= \sigma_h^2\sum_{i=1}^n p_i^2
   = \frac{\sigma_h^2}{n_{\mathrm{eff}}}.
\end{align*}

Thus $n_{\mathrm{eff}}$ is the exact conditional precision-equivalent sample
size for a weighted mean in this setting. It equals $n$ under uniform weights
and decreases as mass is concentrated on fewer observations. Later sections
will identify when this scalar also gives the appropriate normalization for a
dependence statistic and when further weight functionals are required.

\subsection{Weighted ranks and distinct-index sums}

For a marginal variable, define the strict and weak weighted empirical
distribution functions by

\begin{align*}
  F_{X,w}^{-}(x)
  &= \sum_{j=1}^n p_j\,\mathbf{1}(X_j<x),
  &
  F_{X,w}(x)
  &= \sum_{j=1}^n p_j\,\mathbf{1}(X_j\leq x),
\end{align*}

and analogously for $Y$. The strict weighted bivariate rank at observation
$i$ is

\begin{align*}
  H_{i,w}^{-}
  &= \sum_{j=1}^n p_j
     \mathbf{1}(X_j<X_i,\,Y_j<Y_i).
\end{align*}

This rank is the basic computational quantity in the weighted Hoeffding
statistic.

Spearman's coefficient uses a weighted average-rank convention. Let
$G_i^X=\{j:X_j=X_i\}$ be the tie group containing $X_i$, and define its total
mass and pair mass by

\begin{align*}
  P_i^X
  &= \sum_{j\in G_i^X}p_j,
  &
  E_i^X
  &= \sum_{\substack{j<k\\j,k\in G_i^X}}p_jp_k.
\end{align*}

The zero-based average score assigned to every member of this tie group is

\begin{align*}
  R_i^X
  &= F_{X,w}^{-}(X_i)+\frac{E_i^X}{P_i^X}.
\end{align*}

Define $R_i^Y$ in the same way. Under uniform weights, $nR_i^X$ and $nR_i^Y$
are the usual zero-based midranks. With unequal weights, the pair-mass term is
part of the definition and should not be replaced silently by one half of the
mass of the tie group.

Algorithm~\ref{alg:weighted-ranks} computes the strict ranks, weak ranks, and
average scores in one group scan after sorting. For a tie group $G$, its total
mass and pair mass are denoted by $P_G$ and $E_G$, while $C$ is the mass of all
preceding groups.

\begin{algorithm}[ht]
  \caption{Weighted marginal ranks and average scores}
  \label{alg:weighted-ranks}
  \small
  \begin{algorithmic}[1]
    \Procedure{WeightedRanks}{$(X_i,p_i)_{i=1}^n$}
      \State Sort the records by increasing $X_i$
      \State $P_{<G}\gets0$
      \ForAll{maximal tie groups $G$, in increasing order}
        \State $P_G\gets\sum_{j\in G}p_j$
        \State $E_G\gets\bigl(P_G^2-\sum_{j\in G}p_j^2\bigr)/2$
        \ForAll{$i\in G$}
          \State $F_{X,w}^{-}(X_i)\gets P_{<G}$
          \State $F_{X,w}(X_i)\gets P_{<G}+P_G$
          \State $R_i^X\gets P_{<G}+E_G/P_G$
        \EndFor
        \State $P_{<G}\gets P_{<G}+P_G$
      \EndFor
      \State \Return the three score vectors in the original observation order
    \EndProcedure
  \end{algorithmic}
\end{algorithm}

The scan is linear after sorting, so its running time is $O(n\log n)$ and its
auxiliary storage is $O(n)$. The same scan also computes strict rank sums with
unnormalized masses, such as $p_i^a$, by replacing $p_i$ by the desired mass;
normalization is needed only when the output is interpreted as an empirical
distribution function.

Higher-order statistics require sums over distinct observations. For
$k\leq n$, let

\begin{align*}
  e_k(p_1,\ldots,p_n)
  &= \sum_{1\leq i_1<\cdots<i_k\leq n}
     p_{i_1}\cdots p_{i_k}.
\end{align*}

In particular,

\begin{align*}
  e_2(p_1,\ldots,p_n)
  &= \frac{1-\sum_{i=1}^n p_i^2}{2}.
\end{align*}

The quantities $k!e_k$ are the total masses of ordered $k$-tuples of distinct
observations. Section~4 uses Newton's identities to evaluate these terms from
power sums of the weights without enumerating tuples.

\subsection{Asymptotic regime and the role of the weights}

For asymptotic statements the weights form a triangular array, although the
sample index is suppressed when no confusion is possible. The baseline regime
requires

\begin{align*}
  n_{\mathrm{eff}} &\longrightarrow \infty,
  &
  \frac{\max_{1\leq i\leq n}p_i}
       {\sqrt{\sum_{i=1}^n p_i^2}}
  &\longrightarrow 0.
\end{align*}

The second condition is a Lindeberg-type diffuseness requirement: no single
case may contribute a non-negligible fraction of the standard deviation of a
weighted sum. Uniform weights satisfy it, whereas a sequence dominated by a
fixed number of observations does not.

Finite-sample identities in this report are conditional on the observed
weights and need no stochastic model for them. For null distributions, that
model matters. Deterministic weights can be conditioned on directly. If the
weights are functions only of the predictors, independence of $X$ and $Y$
leaves the responses conditionally identically distributed given the
predictors and weights; this is the setting used for the weighted Chatterjee
null calculation. Response-dependent weights invalidate this conditional
exchangeability argument, and weights that depend jointly on both variables
can even induce dependence after reweighting. Such weights require a separate
sampling-design argument and are not covered automatically by the results
below.

Finally, ties caused by atoms in a population distribution are distinct from
accidental equalities in finite-precision data. The formulas below specify
their finite-sample treatment explicitly. Limit results that require
continuous marginals will be labeled as such rather than inferred from the
absence of observed ties.
