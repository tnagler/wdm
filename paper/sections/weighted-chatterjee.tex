\subsection{Weighted Chatterjee's correlation}
\label{sec:weighted-chatterjee}

\subsubsection{Population coefficient and weighted estimator}

Chatterjee's coefficient is directional: it measures the extent to which the
response $Y$ is determined by the predictor $X$ rather than treating the two
variables symmetrically.  If $Y$ is not almost surely constant and $\mu_Y$
denotes its law, the population coefficient is

\begin{align*}
  \xi(X,Y)
  &=
  \frac{
    \displaystyle
    \int
    \operatorname{Var}\!\left\{
      \mathbb{P}(Y\geq t\mid X)
    \right\}\,\mathrm{d}\mu_Y(t)
  }{
    \displaystyle
    \int
    \operatorname{Var}\!\left\{
      \mathbf{1}(Y\geq t)
    \right\}\,\mathrm{d}\mu_Y(t)
  }.
\end{align*}

It belongs to $[0,1]$, equals zero if and only if $X$ and $Y$ are
independent, and equals one if and only if $Y$ is almost surely a measurable
function of $X$ \citep{chatterjee2021}.  In general,
$\xi(X,Y)\neq\xi(Y,X)$.

We now define the case-weighted sample coefficient.  Remove zero-mass
observations, let $m$ be the number remaining, and relabel them so that

\begin{align*}
  X_1\leq X_2\leq\cdots\leq X_m.
\end{align*}

Ties in $X$ are broken uniformly at random without using $Y$; the response
and mass attached to an observation move with it.  Conditional on the
realized ordering, define the weak weighted response rank and survival rank
by

\begin{align*}
  R_{i,w}
  &= \sum_{j=1}^m p_j\mathbf{1}(Y_j\leq Y_i),
  &
  L_{i,w}
  &= \sum_{j=1}^m p_j\mathbf{1}(Y_j\geq Y_i).
\end{align*}

The weighted path variation and response normalizer are

\begin{align*}
  A_w
  &= \sum_{i=1}^{m-1}
     p_i\left|R_{i+1,w}-R_{i,w}\right|,\\
  C_w
  &= 2\sum_{i=1}^m p_iL_{i,w}(1-L_{i,w}),
\end{align*}

and the weighted coefficient is

\begin{align}
  \widehat\xi_w
  &= 1-\frac{A_w}{C_w}.
  \label{eq:weighted-chatterjee}
\end{align}

The base-point mass $p_i$ is attached to the outgoing edge from predictor
position $i$ to $i+1$.  This convention makes a case contribute to the local
path variation in proportion to its empirical mass and, unlike an edge
weight chosen independently of the cases, retains the case-weight
interpretation of Section~\ref{sec:framework}.  The last predictor position
has no outgoing edge.

For uniform masses and an untied response, $R_{i,w}=r_i/m$, where $r_i$ is
the ordinary response rank, and

\begin{align*}
  A_w
  &= \frac{1}{m^2}\sum_{i=1}^{m-1}|r_{i+1}-r_i|,\\
  C_w
  &= \frac{m^2-1}{3m^2}.
\end{align*}

Consequently, \eqref{eq:weighted-chatterjee} reduces exactly to

\begin{align*}
  \widehat\xi
  &= 1-
     \frac{3\sum_{i=1}^{m-1}|r_{i+1}-r_i|}{m^2-1},
\end{align*}

the continuous-response statistic of \citet{chatterjee2021}. For uniform
masses, the same weak-rank formula gives Chatterjee's denominator for response
ties; $C_w$ is its case-weighted extension.

\subsubsection{Finite-sample properties and computation}

The coefficient is invariant under a common positive rescaling of the
original weights and under strictly increasing transformations of either
variable. Because the outgoing edge carries its base-point mass, reversing
the predictor order can change the coefficient when the masses are unequal;
this orientation sensitivity disappears under uniform masses. Zero-mass
observations must be removed before the predictor is
ordered: retaining one would create a spurious edge between two positive-mass
observations.  The coefficient is undefined when the response is constant on
the positive-mass observations, because then $C_w=0$.  Otherwise $C_w>0$ and
$\widehat\xi_w\leq1$, although, like the ordinary finite-sample coefficient,
it can be negative.

Predictor ties need special care.  Ordering a tied predictor group by its
responses would manufacture short response-rank jumps and hence artificial
dependence.  Uniform tie breaking independent of the responses preserves the
null exchangeability used in Section~\ref{sec:independence-tests}.  A fixed
random seed makes the reported coefficient reproducible; averaging over tie
orders would define a different estimator.

Sorting by the predictor and computing the two weighted response ranks by a
second sort takes $O(m\log m)$ time and $O(m)$ auxiliary memory.  The path
variation and normalizer are then obtained in a linear scan.  Equation
\eqref{eq:weighted-chatterjee} is the coefficient reported for both
continuous and tied responses.  Its continuous-response independence test
uses a closely related statistic with a deterministic denominator; that
choice and its null theory are developed entirely in
Section~\ref{sec:independence-tests}.
